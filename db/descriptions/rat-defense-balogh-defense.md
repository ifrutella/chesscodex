The Balogh Defense, also called the Balogh Countergambit, is 1.e4 d6 2.d4 f5: Black strikes at White's e-pawn with the f-pawn at once. The same position comes from the [Staunton Gambit](/openings/dutch-defense-staunton-gambit), 1.d4 f5 2.e4, when Black declines it with 2...d6, and from 1.d4 d6 2.e4 f5. The code is A82.

### Origins

The opening is named after János Balogh (1892–1980), a Hungarian International Master of correspondence chess and a strong over-the-board master, who analysed it in the *Wiener Schachzeitung* in 1930. The moves 1.e4 d6 2.d4 f5 had already been published by Cozio in the first volume of his treatise of 1740, and the line was played in game 5 of the Kieseritzky–Horwitz match in 1846. In a modern example, Hikaru Nakamura used it to beat grandmaster Eugene Perelshteyn at the HB Global Chess Challenge in 2005.

### The idea

Black challenges e4 straight away. The cost is a somewhat weakened kingside, and the structure often leaves Black with a backward e-pawn or a hole on e6 after Black's bishop is exchanged. For these reasons the opening is rarely seen today.

### Main lines

- 3.exf5 Bxf5 4.Qf3 Qc8 5.Bd3 is the line most books give as a refutation, if they mention the opening at all. The correspondence master Keith Hayward, who has argued in a series of articles that the defence is risky but playable, answers with 5...Bg4.
- 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Bd3 is an alternative. Balogh liked 4...Nc6, but Hayward considers 5.exf5 Nxd4 6.g4 difficult for Black and recommends 4...fxe4 5.Nxe4 Nxe4 6.Bxe4 instead.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- Black fights for the centre from move two and avoids the long theory of the [Pirc](/openings/pirc-defense).
- The kingside is loosened, and the e6-square and the e-pawn can become targets.
- White has simple, direct ways to play for an advantage, such as 3.exf5 with Qf3 and Bd3.

### Who should play it

It suits Black players who like the Dutch and want a similar fight against 1.e4, and who are ready to defend a slightly loose position. White should know the 3.exf5 Bxf5 4.Qf3 line, which most books recommend.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 52.9% of 390,290 games and Black 42.8%, with 4.3% drawn. Under 1400 White wins 51.6% and Black 44.6%; between 1400 and 1799 White wins 53.2% and between 1800 and 2199 52.9%. From 2200 up White's share is 54.2% against 39.4% for Black. Only 29 master games are in the database, too few to judge.
