The Amar Opening is 1.Nh3, an irregular first move classified as A00 and also known as the Paris Opening or the Drunken Knight Opening. White develops a knight to the edge of the board, where it controls no central square.

### Origins

The Parisian amateur Charles Amar played the opening in the 1930s. It was probably named by Savielly Tartakower, who used both "Amar" and "Paris" for it. The chess author Tim Harding has jokingly suggested that "Amar" stands for "Absolutely mad and ridiculous". Because 1.Nh3 resembles NH3, the chemical formula for ammonia, it could also be called the Ammonia Opening, in the same way as 1.Na3 is called the [Sodium Attack](/openings/sodium-attack).

World champion Magnus Carlsen used the Amar Opening to defeat Aleksey Dreev in a rapid game in the 2018 online PRO Chess League.

### Main lines

Black's most common reply is 1...d5, which threatens 2...Bxh3 and doubled pawns on White's kingside. White usually plays 2.g3 to prevent this, and Black can continue to occupy the centre with 2...e5.

- 1...d5 2.g3 e5 3.f4 is the [Paris Gambit](/openings/amar-opening-paris-gambit), the best known of the named lines. After 3...Bxh3 4.Bxh3 exf4 White has allowed Black a firm grip on the centre and has given up material as well, so the gambit is considered dubious.
- 5.O-O fxg3 6.hxg3 is the [Gent Gambit](/openings/amar-opening-paris-gambit-gent-gambit), first played by Tartakower against Andor Lilienthal in Paris in 1933.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black wins 48.5% of about 410,000 games and White 46.6%, with 4.9% drawn. Under 1400 Black wins 57.0% and White 35.1%, and between 1400 and 1799 it is 56.8% against 37.6%. Between 1800 and 2199 White is ahead, 49.3% against 46.0%, and from 2200 up Black wins 47.7% and White 45.8%. Only 29 master games are in the database, too few to judge.
