The Kangaroo Defense, better known as the Keres Defence, is 1.d4 e6 2.c4 Bb4+: after 1...e6 Black checks with the bishop on b4, and the game often transposes to a better-known opening. It is named after the Estonian grandmaster Paul Keres; the code is A40.

### Origins

The opening has been known since the 1840s. Henry Thomas Buckle played it in his fourth match game against Johann Löwenthal in London in 1851. Howard Staunton recommended 3.Bd2, which is the standard reply today.

### The idea

White can answer the check with 3.Nc3, 3.Nd2 or 3.Bd2, and the game often transposes to a [Nimzo-Indian](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense), a [Dutch](/openings/dutch-defense), a [Queen's Gambit Declined](/openings/queens-gambit-declined), an [English Defence](/openings/english-defense) or a [Bogo-Indian](/openings/bogo-indian-defense).

### Main lines

- 3.Nc3 ([Transpositional Variation](/openings/kangaroo-defense-keres-defense-transpositional-variation)) usually leads into another opening: 3...Nf6 is a Nimzo-Indian, 3...f5 a Dutch, 3...d5 an unusual form of the Queen's Gambit Declined and 3...b6 an English Defence. Korn gives 3...Bxc3+ 4.bxc3 f5, the line Buckle played.
- 3.Nd2 gives Black the same options, except that 3...Nf6 4.Nf3 is a Bogo-Indian.
- 3.Bd2 is the main move. 3...Bxd2+ goes into a Bogo-Indian line, and 3...a5 usually transposes to one once White plays Nf3. Black can also allow e2–e4 with 3...Qe7 4.e4. After 4...d5 5.Bxb4 Qxb4+ 6.Qd2 Qxd2+ 7.Nxd2 White is slightly better, and 6...Nc6 is met by 7.Nc3. 4...Nf6 5.a3 Bxd2+ 6.Nxd2 d6 7.Bd3 e5 8.d5 O-O gave Black a good game in Llanos–Hoffman, San Luis Clarin 1995. On move 5 White can also play 5.e5, as in Timman–Spraggett, Montpellier 1985.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- Black keeps a wide choice of structures and can steer the game towards the defence they know best.
- The bishop check is often followed by an exchange on d2 or c3, so Black gives up the bishop pair in many lines.
- Against 3.Bd2 Qe7 4.e4 White gets a full pawn centre, and the endgame after the queen exchange is slightly better for White.

### Who should play it

It suits Black players who already play the Nimzo-Indian, the Bogo-Indian or the Dutch and want to reach them while avoiding some of White's options. White should know 3.Bd2, Staunton's move, and be ready to play e2–e4 when Black allows it.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 52.4% of 568,172 games and Black 42.3%, with 5.2% drawn. White wins 55.7% under 1400 and 55.5% between 1400 and 1799, then 51.2% between 1800 and 2199. From 2200 up the sides are level at 45.6% each, with 8.8% drawn. In 3,711 master games White wins 32.5%, Black 24.7%, and 42.8% are drawn.
