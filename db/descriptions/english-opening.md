The English Opening (1.c4) is a flexible first move in which White controls d5 from the side instead of occupying the centre with a pawn. It often transposes into 1.d4 openings, and it can also lead to its own positions, such as a Sicilian with an extra tempo for White.

### Origins

It is called the English Opening because the English master Howard Staunton played it in his 1843 match against Pierre Saint-Amant and in the 1851 London tournament. It did not catch on with Staunton's contemporaries and became popular only in the 20th century. Today it is regarded as a solid opening that can lead to both classical and hypermodern positions. World champions Mikhail Botvinnik, Garry Kasparov and Magnus Carlsen have used it in their world championship matches, and Ding Liren played it in his title matches of 2023 and 2024. Bobby Fischer created a stir when he switched to it from his customary 1.e4, and he played 1.c4 against Boris Spassky in their 1972 match.

### The idea

White fights for the centre by staking a claim to d5 from the wing. One common set-up is the Botvinnik System, with c4, Nc3, d3, e4, g3, Bg2 and Ne2 in some order; White can also play Nf3, g3 and Bg2 and transpose into a Réti. The English keeps a wide choice of structures: reversed Sicilian, symmetrical, or 1.d4 transpositions, and its many move orders make Black choose carefully how to reach a favourite defence.

### Main lines

- [King's English Variation](/openings/english-opening-kings-english-variation) 1...e5: the [Reversed Sicilian](/openings/english-opening-kings-english-variation-reversed-sicilian), where White plays a Sicilian with colours reversed, a tempo up. It includes the [Four Knights](/openings/english-opening-kings-english-variation-four-knights-variation) and [Reversed Closed Sicilian](/openings/english-opening-kings-english-variation-reversed-closed-sicilian) setups.
- [Symmetrical Variation](/openings/english-opening-symmetrical-variation) 1...c5: Black copies White's c-pawn move, and either side may break early in the centre with the d-pawn.
- [Anglo-Indian Defense](/openings/english-opening-anglo-indian-defense) 1...Nf6: the most common reply; Black usually aims to transpose into an Indian Defence, or into the [Hedgehog](/openings/english-opening-anglo-indian-defense-hedgehog-system) setups.
- [Agincourt Defense](/openings/english-opening-agincourt-defense) 1...e6 and the [Caro-Kann setup](/openings/english-opening-caro-kann-defensive-system) 1...c6: Black prepares ...d5.
- [Anglo-Scandinavian](/openings/english-opening-anglo-scandinavian-defense) 1...d5 and the [Anglo-Dutch](/openings/english-opening-anglo-dutch-defense) 1...f5.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Very flexible, with fewer forced lines to memorise than 1.e4 or 1.d4.
- Lets White choose the structure and avoid an opponent's favourite defence.
- Positions tend to be strategic, rewarding understanding over preparation.

**Weaknesses**

- Less direct: Black equalises quite easily with sensible development.
- Move-order tricks cut both ways. You must know how to answer each transposition.
- Not ideal for players who want early attacks and tactics.

### Who should play it

The English suits positional players who like to manoeuvre and to steer the game into familiar structures. It is effective against opponents whose repertoire is built on sharp theory against 1.e4 or 1.d4, since it avoids their preparation. It gives the least against players who meet it with a solid symmetrical setup and are happy to make a draw.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 50–51% and Black 44.3–45.5% below 2200. Above 2200, White still leads 49% to 43%. In master games over the board, White wins 34%, Black 22.5% and 44% are drawn.
