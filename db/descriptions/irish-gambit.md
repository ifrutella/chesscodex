The Irish Gambit is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Nxe5: White takes the e5-pawn with the knight although the knight on c6 defends it, and gives up a piece for a pawn. The code is C44. The Chicago Gambit, 3...Nxe5 4.d4, is derived from it.

### Origins

The chess historian Edward Winter traced the gambit to an article in the *Columbia Chess Chronicle* of July 16, 1887. Its author claims that an Irishman named Dennis O'Flaherty invented the gambit, and ends with the inventor's deathbed explanation of the sacrifice: "I did not see that the Pawn was protected." *The Oxford Companion to Chess* tells a similar story about an anonymous inventor. In 2018 the writer Shawn Gillen published an autobiographical essay called *The Irish Gambit*, which begins by presenting the gambit as an ethnic joke.

### The Chicago Gambit

*The Oxford Companion to Chess* defines the Chicago Gambit as 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Nxe5 Nxe5 4.d4, with the pawn attacking the knight on e5. A doctor named D. T. Phillips used it to beat Harry Nelson Pillsbury in a simultaneous exhibition in Chicago on January 7, 1899. The *Companion* calls the Chicago Gambit "unsound" and compares it unfavourably with the Müller–Schulze Gambit, also known as the [Halloween Gambit](/openings/four-knights-game-halloween-gambit) (1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.Nxe5). On this site 4.d4 is listed as the [Schulze-Müller Gambit](/openings/kings-pawn-game-schulze-muller-gambit), the Lichess name.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- White gets a pawn for the knight and gains a tempo with d2–d4.
- A piece for a pawn is a lot to give; even the Chicago Gambit is called "unsound".
- Black's moves are natural: take the knight and move it out of the way of the d-pawn.

### Who should play it

It is a surprise weapon for casual games at most. Black needs no preparation: take the knight with 3...Nxe5, meet 4.d4 by moving the knight to safety, and develop.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black wins 56.5% of the 87,669 games and White 40.4%, with 3.1% drawn.
