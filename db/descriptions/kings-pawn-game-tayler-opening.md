The Tayler Opening, also called the Inverted Hungarian Opening, is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Be2. The second name comes from the bishop on e2, which stands where Black's bishop goes on e7 in the [Hungarian Defense](/openings/italian-game-hungarian-defense). The code is C44.

### Origins

The opening is also known as the Inverted Hanham, and its best-known appearance is Tartakower's game against Bogoljubow at London 1922. After 3...Nf6, John Tayler introduced the gambit 4.d4 and published his analysis in *Chess*, February–March 1981. Michael Basman took the gambit up and developed it further.

### The idea

The Inverted Hungarian is even rarer than the Hungarian Defense, which is itself uncommon, but it is perfectly playable for White. Because White has the first move, there is more room to play a move that is not objectively the strongest without ending up worse. 3.Be2 makes no threat, so Black has many good replies. If White continues with the set-up Black uses in the Hanham Variation of the [Philidor Defense](/openings/philidor-defense-hanham-variation), 3...Nf6 4.d3 d5 5.Nbd2, the line is sometimes called the [Inverted Hanham Opening](/openings/kings-pawn-game-tayler-opening-inverted-hanham).

### Main lines

- 3...Nf6 4.d3 d5 5.Nbd2 is the quiet Inverted Hanham set-up.
- 3...Nf6 4.d4 exd4 5.e5 Ng4 is the critical line of Tayler's gambit, the [Basman Gambit](/openings/kings-pawn-game-tayler-opening-basman-gambit). Play continues 6.O-O Be7 7.Nxd4 Ngxe5 8.f4 Nxd4 9.Qxd4 Ng6 10.f5 Bf6 11.Qf2 Ne7; 10.Qxg7 Bf6 11.Qh6 is another try, and 6.Bf4 d6 is an alternative on move 6.
- In the same line 6...Ngxe5 7.Nxe5 Nxe5 8.Qxd4 Nc6 9.Qc3 leaves Black with problems developing the kingside, according to Basman.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- White steers clear of heavily analysed double king's pawn openings such as the [Ruy Lopez](/openings/ruy-lopez).
- The bishop on e2 is solid but passive, and 3.Be2 does nothing to stop Black's natural development.
- The 4.d4 gambit gives White a second, sharper way to play the same first moves.

### Who should play it

It may appeal to White players who want to avoid well-analysed openings, and to those who like defensive, manoeuvring battles of the kind the Hungarian Defense often produces. Black can simply develop: 3.Be2 threatens nothing, so moves like ...Nf6 and ...d5 come easily.

### The numbers

On Lichess the results are even: White wins 47.5% of 991,312 games and Black 47.8%, with 4.8% drawn. Under 1400 Black wins 48.0% against 47.9%, between 1400 and 1799 White is slightly ahead at 48.4% against 47.3%, and between 1800 and 2199 Black leads 48.6% to 46.4%. From 2200 up Black wins 47.1% and White 45.9%. In 278 master games White wins 31.3%, Black 29.9%, and 38.8% are drawn.
