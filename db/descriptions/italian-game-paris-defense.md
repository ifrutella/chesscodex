The Paris Defense is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 d6, an uncommon reply to the [Italian Game](/openings/italian-game). It is also called the Semi-Italian Opening, the Half Giuoco Piano and the Lesser Giuoco Piano, and its code is C50. Black props up e5 with the d-pawn and keeps the bishop on c8 free for ...Bg4.

### Origins

Alexander Alekhine tried the line early in his career. In Rodzinski–Alekhine, Paris 1913, White went after material and was mated on move 15: 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.Bc4 d6 4.c3 Bg4 5.Qb3 Qd7 6.Ng5 Nh6 7.Nxf7 Nxf7 8.Bxf7+ Qxf7 9.Qxb7 Kd7 10.Qxa8 Qc4 11.f3 Bxf3 12.gxf3 Nd4 13.d3 Qxd3 14.cxd4 Be7 15.Qxh8 Bh4#. According to Alekhine, 6.Bxf7+ Qxf7 7.Qxb7 Kd7 8.Qxa8 Bxf3 9.gxf3 Qxf3 10.Rg1 Qxe4+ 11.Kd1 Qf3+ 12.Ke1 Qe4+ would have ended in perpetual check.

I. A. Horowitz called the defence "solid", but also wrote: "It does not seem quite sufficient for equality."

### The idea

Black wants a [Hungarian Defense](/openings/italian-game-hungarian-defense) set-up with an early ...Bg4, fighting for control of d4. The same position arises from the [Philidor Defense](/openings/philidor-defense) after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 d6 3.Bc4 Nc6.

### Main lines

- 4.c3 is the main line. In Levenfish–Tolush, Leningrad 1939, White was slightly better after 4...Bg4 5.d4 Qe7 6.Be3 Nf6 7.Qb3 Nd8 8.Nbd2 g6 9.dxe5; 5.d4 is better than Rodzinski's 5.Qb3. Alekhine judged 4...Qe7 followed by ...g6 satisfactory for Black, and Tartakower preferred 4...Be6.
- 4.d4 Bg4 5.c3 gives White an edge in most lines: 5...Qd7 leaves White some space advantage, and 5...Nf6 6.Qb3 is clearly better for White according to Keres. 5...Qe7 transposes to the 4.c3 line. 5.h3 Bxf3 6.Qxf3 also leaves White slightly better. After 4...exd4 5.Nxd4 Nf6 6.Nc3 White has the freer game according to Keres, and 4...Be7 transposes to the Hungarian Defense.
- 4.h3 stops ...Bg4 and leads to positions like those of the Hungarian Defense, for example after 4...Be7 5.d4.
- 4.Nc3 Bg4 5.h3 Bxf3 6.Qxf3 Nf6 7.Ne2 (or 7.d3) leaves White slightly better, as in Maslov–Lutikov, USSR 1963.

### The Légal Trap

4.Nc3 Bg4 5.h3 Bh5? is the natural route into the Légal Trap: 6.Nxe5 gives up the queen, and 6...Bxd1 7.Bxf7+ Ke7 8.Nd5# is mate by three minor pieces. Black's best is 6...Nxe5, and after 7.Qxh5 Nxc4 8.Qb5+ and 9.Qxc4 White is only a pawn up. On move 5 Black does better with 5...Bxf3 or 5...Bd7, and 5...Be6 is also possible. The trap is named after the French player Sire de Legall, and Joseph Henry Blackburne set it on many occasions.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- The set-up is solid and easy to play: ...d6, ...Bg4 and a fight for d4.
- White gets more space in most lines, and Black's king's bishop usually settles on e7, a more modest square than c5 in the Giuoco Piano.
- The ...Bg4 pin has a tactical flaw after 4.Nc3 and 5.h3: a retreat to h5 walks into the Légal Trap.

### Who should play it

It suits Black players who want a quiet position against 3.Bc4 and are happy to defend a slightly worse game. White players should know the Légal Trap: 5...Bh5 loses at least a pawn even when Black avoids the mate.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 51.5% of 8.4 million games and Black 43.9%, with 4.6% drawn. The figures hardly change between rating bands: White wins 51.8% under 1400, 51.5% between 1400 and 1799, 51.7% between 1800 and 2199 and 50.3% from 2200 up. In 701 master games White wins 37.2%, Black 29.7%, and 33.1% are drawn.
