The Dutch Defense (1.d4 f5) is an aggressive answer to 1.d4. By playing ...f5, Black fights for the e4-square immediately and prepares a kingside attack, creating an unbalanced position from the very first move.

### Origins

The opening is named after Elias Stein (1748–1812), an Alsatian who settled in The Hague, and who recommended 1...f5 as the best reply to 1.d4 in his 1789 book. Its most notable use came in the 1951 world championship match, in which both Mikhail Botvinnik and his challenger David Bronstein played it. Other famous practitioners include Alexander Alekhine, Miguel Najdorf and Bent Larsen. In recent years Hikaru Nakamura has played it, and Magnus Carlsen has used the Stonewall to beat Viswanathan Anand and Fabiano Caruana.

### The idea

1...f5 stakes a claim to e4 and, like the Sicilian against 1.e4, unbalances the game. The cost is king safety: the e8–h5 diagonal is weakened, and Black must watch out for early attacks. Black's plans depend on the setup:

- **Stonewall**: pawns on c6, d5, e6 and f5 give a tight grip on e4, but leave a hole on e5.
- **Leningrad**: ...g6, ...Bg7 and ...d6; the bishop on g7 defends the king and is active on the long diagonal.
- **Classical**: ...e6 and ...Be7, after which Black chooses between ...d5, ...d6 and ...Ne4.

### Main lines

- [Stonewall Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-stonewall-variation) with ...d5 and a tight grip on e4.
- [Leningrad Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-leningrad-variation) with ...g6 and the bishop on g7.
- [Classical Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-classical-variation) with ...e6 and ...d6.

White can avoid these structures with the [Staunton Gambit](/openings/dutch-defense-staunton-gambit) 2.e4, the [Hopton Attack](/openings/dutch-defense-hopton-attack) 2.Bg5, or the [Raphael Variation](/openings/dutch-defense-raphael-variation) 2.Nc3. Black must know how to handle all of them.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Unbalanced from the first move, so Black plays for a win against 1.d4.
- Clear strategic plans: the fight for e4 and kingside expansion with ...g5 or ...f4.
- Less mainstream than the [Queen's Gambit Declined](/openings/queens-gambit-declined) or the [King's Indian](/openings/kings-indian-defense), so opponents are often less prepared.

**Weaknesses**

- Weakens Black's king, especially the e8–h5 diagonal and the e6-square.
- White has several aggressive anti-Dutch lines (the Staunton Gambit, 2.Bg5, the Korchnoi Attack with h3 and g4).
- The Stonewall leaves a permanent hole on e5, which White can occupy with a knight.

### Who should play it

The Dutch suits ambitious players who want a fighting, unbalanced game against 1.d4 and like kingside attacks. It is effective against positional players who expect a quiet Queen's Gambit and don't know the Dutch structures. It is least comfortable against well-prepared opponents who choose an anti-Dutch system, or who calmly occupy e5 against the Stonewall.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 50–51% and Black 45–46% below 2200, and 50% against 43% above it. In master games over the board, White wins 36%, Black 23% and 41% are drawn.
