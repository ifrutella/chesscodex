The Gunderam Defense is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Qe7, a rarely played way to defend the e5-pawn with the queen. With ...g6, ...Bg7 and ...Nf6 to follow it is also called the Brazilian Defense or the Câmara Defense. The code is C40.

### Origins

The opening is named after the player and theoretician Gerhart Gunderam, although Hélder Câmara played it four years before him. Câmara, an International Master, first used it in 1954, in the IV Centennial of the City of São Paulo tournament and the XXII Brazilian Championship. It became popular among Brazilian players, who called it "the Brazilian defense" and used it often in the national championship in the following years; Câmara later asked for it to be called the Câmara Defense.

Gunderam first played it in a correspondence match against August Babel in 1958 and published his analysis in the book *Neue Eröffnungswege* in 1961, under the name "Damenverteidigung" ("Queen Defence"). In 1969 Washington de Oliveira published *Notas Sobre a Defesa Brasileira* ("Annotations on the Brazilian Defense"), a study of Câmara's games with it.

### The idea

2...Qe7 meets the threat to e5, but the queen stands in the way of Black's dark-squared bishop. One idea behind the awkward queen move is to unbalance the game by castling queenside while White presumably castles kingside. The two namesakes had different plans: Gunderam aimed for the break ...f7–f5, a delayed [Latvian Gambit](/openings/latvian-gambit), while Câmara wanted a King's Indian set-up against 1.e4 with ...g6, ...Bg7 and ...Nf6.

### Main lines

- Gunderam's main line was 3.Nc3 c6 4.Bc4 f5.
- He also analysed the sharp 3.Bc4 f5 ([Gunderam Gambit](/openings/kings-pawn-game-gunderam-defense-gunderam-gambit)) 4.exf5 d5 5.Bxd5 Nf6 6.Bb3 Bxf5.
- Câmara's plan is the King's Indian formation: ...g6, ...Bg7 and ...Nf6 after 2...Qe7.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- Black leaves the main lines of 2...Nc6 behind and can choose between a quiet King's Indian set-up and the sharp ...f5 break.
- The queen on e7 blocks the bishop on f8 until it moves again or the bishop goes to g7.
- Opposite-side castling makes the game sharp for both sides.

### Who should play it

It suits Black players who already play the King's Indian and want a similar structure against 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3, or who like the ...f5 break of the Latvian without giving up a pawn at once. White can develop normally and aim the pieces at Black's centre while the queen blocks the f8-bishop.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 50.3% of 611,591 games and Black 45.6%, with 4.1% drawn. White does best in the lower bands: 55.5% under 1400 and 53.5% between 1400 and 1799. Between 1800 and 2199 the sides are close, 48.3% for White and 47.4% for Black, and from 2200 up White wins 48.5% and Black 46.5%. In 301 master games White wins 44.9%, Black 24.3%, and 30.9% are drawn.
