The Scandinavian Defense (1.e4 d5), also called the Centre Counter, is arguably Black's most forcing defence to 1.e4. Black challenges the e4-pawn immediately. After 2.exd5 Black recaptures, usually with the queen, hoping for a free game with easy development at the cost of a tempo.

### Origins

The Scandinavian is one of the oldest recorded openings. It appears in a fictional game between Francesc de Castellví and Narcís Vinyoles in the Catalan poem *Scachs d'amor*, written around 1475. That game features the retreat 3...Qd8, and in 2023 two Spanish authors proposed naming that line the [Valencian Variation](/openings/scandinavian-defense-valencian-variation) in its honour. The opening also appears in Lucena's chess book of 1497. Long known as the Centre Counter, it was shown to be playable by Scandinavian masters in the late 19th century. The name "Scandinavian" acknowledges the Swedish player Ludvig Collijn, who played it with success. Its modern popularity grew after Bent Larsen beat Anatoly Karpov with it in 1979. Viswanathan Anand obtained an excellent position with it against Garry Kasparov in their 1995 world championship match, and Magnus Carlsen has chosen it against Fabiano Caruana.

### The idea

After 2.exd5 Qxd5 3.Nc3, Black's queen is chased and White gains a tempo. That is the price Black pays. In return, Black gets a clear structure: in the main line, pawns on c6 and e6 with the light-squared bishop developed to f5 before ...e6. The structure resembles the [Caro-Kann](/openings/caro-kann-defense), and many Caro-Kann players have taken up this form of the Scandinavian.

### Main lines

- [Main Line](/openings/scandinavian-defense-main-line) 3...Qa5: the main line and the most popular retreat, keeping the queen active on the a5–e1 diagonal. White's options include the [4.d4 Nf6 line](/openings/scandinavian-defense-main-line-mieses-variation).
- [Gubinsky-Melts Defense](/openings/scandinavian-defense-gubinsky-melts-defense) 3...Qd6: long poorly regarded, it has been played many times in high-level chess since the mid-1990s.
- [Valencian Variation](/openings/scandinavian-defense-valencian-variation) 3...Qd8: the old main line, still playable but considered somewhat passive.
- [Modern Variation](/openings/scandinavian-defense-modern-variation) 2...Nf6: Black delays the recapture, and the [Portuguese Gambit](/openings/scandinavian-defense-portuguese-gambit) 3.d4 Bg4 or the [Icelandic-Palme Gambit](/openings/scandinavian-defense-icelandic-palme-gambit) 3.c4 e6 give sharp play.

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Very little theory, and Black chooses the type of position from move one.
- Easy development and a solid structure. It is hard for White to find a clear target.
- Avoids the huge theory of the [Sicilian](/openings/sicilian-defense), [French](/openings/french-defense) and Ruy Lopez.

**Weaknesses**

- Black loses time with the queen, so White gets a small lead in development.
- Black's position can be passive, and winning against solid opponents takes patience.
- At the highest level it is considered less sound than the main defences.

### Who should play it

The Scandinavian suits practical players who want a reliable, low-theory defence to 1.e4 and like clear structures. It works especially well against opponents who rely on preparation in the main lines, and against players who don't know how to use their lead in development. Its weakest point is against well-prepared White players who develop quickly and play for a lasting space advantage.

### The numbers

On Lichess the Scandinavian is close to even at club level: White wins 48–50% and Black 46–47% at every band below 2200. Among masters the picture changes. Over the board, White wins 38.5%, Black 24.5%, and 37% are drawn.
