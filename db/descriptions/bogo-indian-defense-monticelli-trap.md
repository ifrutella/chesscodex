The Monticelli Trap is a combination in the [Bogo-Indian Defence](/openings/bogo-indian-defense): 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 Bb4+ 4.Bd2 Bxd2+ 5.Qxd2 b6 6.g3 Bb7 7.Bg2 O-O 8.Nc3 Ne4 9.Qc2 Nxc3 10.Ng5!. On this site the line is filed under the [Queen's Indian](/openings/queens-indian-defense) move order 3...b6 4.g3 Bb7 5.Bg2 Bb4+, which reaches the same position. The code is E11.

### Origins

The trap is named after the Italian champion Mario Monticelli, from his game against Prokeš at Budapest in 1926. It is called a trap because White wins the exchange, but Black does get some compensation. Former world champion José Raúl Capablanca allowed 10.Ng5 as Black in two consecutive games against Max Euwe in Amsterdam in 1931 and drew both. The second went 10...Ne4 11.Bxe4 Bxe4 12.Qxe4 Qxg5 13.Qxa8 Nc6 14.Qb7 Nxd4 15.Rd1 c5 16.e3 Nc2+ 17.Kd2 Qf5 18.Qg2 Nb4 19.e4 Qf6 20.Kc1 Nxa2+ 21.Kb1 Nb4 22.Rxd7 Nc6 23.f4 e5 24.Rhd1 Nd4 25.Rxa7 exf4 26.gxf4 Qxf4 27.Re1 Nf3 28.Re2 Nd4 29.Re1, ½–½.

### The idea

After 10.Ng5 White threatens mate with 11.Qxh7# and also 11.Bxb7, which wins a bishop and a rook. After 10...Ne4 11.Bxe4 or 10...Qxg5 11.Bxb7 Black loses the exchange but gets one or more pawns for it, and sometimes a weakened white king. It is unclear whether White is winning by force.

### At the top level

The line has been played several times at the highest level, for example in Portisch–Andersson 1983, a draw, and Aronian–Postny 2005, which White won. White has also turned down the exchange in grandmaster games, either with 10.Qxc3 or with 10.Ng5 Ne4 11.Bxe4 Bxe4 12.Nxe4, as in Psakhis–Marin 2005.

### Who should know it

Bogo-Indian and Queen's Indian players with Black should know that ...Ne4 and ...Nxc3 with the bishop on b7 allows Ng5. With White, the combination wins the exchange, but Black's pawns and play against the king can be enough for a draw, as Capablanca showed twice.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 55.2% of the 268 games and Black 31.0%, with 13.8% drawn.
