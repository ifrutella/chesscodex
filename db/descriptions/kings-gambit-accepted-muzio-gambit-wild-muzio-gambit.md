The Muzio Gambit, sometimes called the Polerio Gambit, is 1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.Bc4 g4 5.O-O, a line of the [King's Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit). White leaves the knight on f3 to be taken and castles, giving up a piece for a big lead in development and pressure on the f-file. The Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings classifies it as C37; the Lichess name of this line is the Wild Muzio Gambit.

### Origins

Giulio Cesare Polerio analysed the opening in the late 16th century. The first recorded game is by the Neapolitan player Geronimo Cascio, in Alessandro Salvio's *Il Puttino* of 1634. The name "Muzio Gambit" comes from the early 19th-century English writer Jacob Sarratt, who in his translation of *Il Puttino* wrongly credited the opening to Cascio's contemporary Mutio d'Allesandro. In its original form White used Italian-style free castling, with the king on h1 and the rook on f1, which made the attack even stronger: Black could no longer gain time with a check on the g1–a7 diagonal.

The gambit was at its most popular in the mid-19th century, the Romantic era, when sacrifices and early attacks were seen as the height of chess art. It declined as defensive technique improved, in the hands of players such as Louis Paulsen and Wilhelm Steinitz.

### The idea

4.Bc4 rather than 4.h4 usually shows that White intends the Muzio. White aims at Black's weakness on the f-file. Black can avoid the whole line with 4...Bg7, often recommended as a safe practical choice. On move 5 White has other piece sacrifices, the [Salvio Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-salvio-gambit) 5.Ne5, the [Lolli Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-lolli-gambit) 5.Bxf7+, the [McDonnell Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-mcdonnell-gambit) 5.Nc3 and the [Ghulam Kassim Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-ghulam-kassim-gambit) 5.d4, but 5.O-O is generally reckoned the strongest. The Salvio has almost disappeared because of 5...Qh4+.

### Main lines

- 5...gxf3 6.Qxf3 Qf6 ([Sarratt Defense](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-muzio-gambit-sarratt-defense)) is the thematic starting position. 6...Qf6 has been called "very definitely best", since it blocks the f-file and hinders a white pawn centre with d4. 7.e5, "the most logical" move, gives up a pawn as well to open new lines, and after 7...Qxe5 White chooses between 8.d3 and 8.Bxf7+.
- 8.d3 Bh6 9.Nc3 Ne7 10.Bd2 Nbc6 11.Rae1 Qf5 is the main line, the Paulsen Variation. White was thought to be better until a Milwaukee player identified only as "W.S." suggested 11...Qf5 in 1858; before that 11...Qc5+ was played. Paulsen used the move in his 1861 match with Ignatz Kolisch and won the game. After 12.Nd5 Kd8, 13.Qe2 Qe6 14.Qf2 Qf5 is a draw by repetition, and 13.Bc3 gives Black three good squares for the rook.
- 8.Bxf7+ Kxf7 9.d4 is the [Double Muzio Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-double-muzio-gambit), "the best version of the Muzio" according to Keene. It is very dangerous against an unprepared opponent, but its soundness has been questioned. 9...Qxd4+ 10.Be3 Qf6 11.Bxf4 was the traditional choice, which Peter Millican calls "objectively equal"; John Shaw sharply criticises 9...Qxd4+, and Neil McDonald suggests that 9...Qf5 may be the only playable move.
- 6...Qe7 ([From's Defense](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-muzio-gambit-accepted-froms-defense)) is met best by 7.d4 Nc6 8.Nc3, as in the casual game Steinitz–Anderssen, London 1862. Black can also delay taking the knight with 5...d5 ([Brentano Defense](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-muzio-gambit-brentano-defense)), after which White gets a strong attack, or play 5...Qe7.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- White gets a large lead in development and lasting pressure on the f-file for the knight.
- Black has well-tested defences: the main line with 11...Qf5 has been known since 1858, and several lines end in a draw by repetition.
- Black can sidestep the sacrifice completely with 4...Bg7.

### Who should play it

It suits attacking White players who are happy to play a piece down and know the main lines after 6...Qf6. As Black, either learn the defence with 6...Qf6 and ...Qf5 or avoid the gambit with 4...Bg7.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 54.6% of 407,832 games and Black 42.6%, with only 2.7% drawn. White does best lower down: 61.6% under 1400, 58.3% between 1400 and 1799 and 53.3% between 1800 and 2199. From 2200 up the sides are close, 48.8% for White and 47.7% for Black. Only 44 master games are in the database, too few to judge.
