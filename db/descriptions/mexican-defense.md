The Mexican Defense is 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 Nc6, an opening better known today as the Black Knights' Tango. Black develops both knights first and is ready to strike back in the centre with ...e5, or with ...e6 and ...d5. It is also called the Two Knights' Tango and the Kevitz–Trajkovic Defense.

### Origins

The opening originated in the 1920s, when it was played by the Mexican grandmaster Carlos Torre, which explains the name Mexican Defense, and by the American master Alexander Kevitz. Torre used it to defeat the U.S. champion Frank Marshall in only seven moves. It was later played by the Yugoslav master Mihailo Trajkovic and the Soviet grandmaster Anatoly Lutikov.

After decades of obscurity the opening was revitalized by International Master Georgi Orlov, who published a booklet about it in 1992 and a book in 1998 and gave it the name Black Knights' Tango. Since 1992 it has been used by a number of strong grandmasters, including Victor Bologan, Joel Benjamin, Larry Christiansen and Alex Yermolinsky. Yermolinsky even ventured it against Garry Kasparov.

### The idea

The Tango is fairly uncommon, but it has a sounder positional basis than most other offbeat openings: Black develops quickly, has a flexible pawn structure and is prepared to strike back in the centre. It has some variations of its own but is highly transpositional. It may turn into the [King's Indian Defense](/openings/kings-indian-defense), the [Nimzo-Indian Defense](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense), the [Bogo-Indian Defense](/openings/bogo-indian-defense), the [Chigorin Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-chigorin-defense), the [Ragozin](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-ragozin-defense), the [Catalan Opening](/openings/catalan-opening) or the [English Opening](/openings/english-opening).

The position after 2...Nc6 can also be reached by other move orders, for example 1.c4 Nf6, 1.d4 Nc6 or 1.c4 Nc6.

### Main lines

- 3.Nf3 is the most common move and prevents 3...e5. Black usually responds with 3...e6, although 3...d6, heading for a kind of Old Indian Defense, is also possible. After 3...e6 White has three main moves. 4.Nc3 Bb4 transposes to the Nimzo-Indian. 4.a3 can be met by 4...d5, a kind of Queen's Gambit Declined or Ragozin, or by 4...d6, which prepares ...e5 or ...g6 with a sort of King's Indian. After 4.g3 Black can transpose to the Catalan with 4...d5 or play 4...Bb4+, which becomes a Nimzo-Indian after 5.Nc3 and a Bogo-Indian after 5.Bd2 or 5.Nbd2.
- 3.Nc3 is White's second most popular move. After the thematic 3...e5 White can play 4.Nf3, transposing to an English Opening, but the main line is 4.d5 Ne7. The game may then continue in Tango fashion with 5.Nf3 Ng6, or transpose with 5.Nf3 d6 6.e4 g6 7.Be2 Bg7 8.O-O O-O to the main line of the King's Indian.
- 3.d5 is ambitious and playable but rarely seen. Black normally responds with 3...Ne5. After 4.e4 Black must not take the pawn, because 4...Nxe4 5.Qd4 wins a knight. In Sämisch–Torre, Moscow 1925, Black played 4...Ng6 5.f4 e5. Orlov considers both of those moves inferior and, like Richard Palliser, recommends 4...e6, after which play can become extremely sharp. 4.f4 is the [Horsefly Gambit](/openings/mexican-defense-horsefly-gambit).

### Who should play it

- The opening suits players who already know several Indian defences, because many of its lines end up in one of them.
- It is a way to take 1.d4 players out of their preparation on move two without playing an unsound line.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black wins 48.1% of about 664,000 games and White 47.0%, with 4.9% drawn. Under 1400 White wins 55.5% and Black 40.7%, and between 1400 and 1799 it is 55.0% against 41.4%. Between 1800 and 2199 Black is ahead, 50.0% against 45.3%, and from 2200 up Black wins 48.8% and White 44.0%. In 2,041 master games over the board White won 38.6%, Black 28.4%, and 33.0% were drawn.
