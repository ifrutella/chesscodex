The Blumenfeld Countergambit, or Blumenfeld Gambit, is 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 c5 4.d5 b5. Black gives up a wing pawn to build a big centre with pawns on c5, d5 and e6. It is a variation of the [Benoni Defense](/openings/benoni-defense), and the Encyclopaedia of Chess Openings classifies it under E10.

### Origins

The opening is named after the Russian master Benjamin Blumenfeld, and World Champion Alexander Alekhine played it later. The basic order is 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 c5 3.d5 e6 4.Nf3 b5, but many others are possible. The order on this page is common when White avoids the [Nimzo-Indian](/openings/nimzo-indian-defense) (2...e6 3.Nc3 Bb4) with 3.Nf3. The [Benko Gambit](/openings/benko-gambit) can also transpose after 3...b5 4.Nf3 e6, although White usually does not play 4.Nf3 there.

### The idea

Black develops the bishops to b7 and d6 and uses the half-open f-file for a rook, which helps Black's play on the kingside. White usually looks for counterplay in the centre with e4 at some point, and the extra queenside pawn gives White some initiative on that side as well.

### Main lines

- 5.Bg5 is the most frequent move ([Duz-Khotimirsky Variation](/openings/blumenfeld-countergambit-duz-khotimirsky-variation)); it was seen in Vaganian–K. Grigorian, 1971. The usual reply is 5...exd5 6.cxd5, and then 6...h6 7.Bxf6 Qxf6 8.Qc2 or 6...d6 7.e4 a6 8.a4 Be7. Black can also play 5...b4, usually followed by 6.e4 d6.
- 5.dxe6 fxe6 6.cxb5 accepts the gambit ([Blumenfeld Countergambit Accepted](/openings/blumenfeld-countergambit-accepted) after 6...d5), as in Kan–Goldenov, 1946. After 6...d5 White has an extra pawn but Black dominates the centre; White's most common seventh moves are 7.Nc3, 7.g3 and 7.e3. 6...a6 is an alternative, inviting 7.bxa6 Bxa6, but White may decline the exchange, most often with 7.e3.
- 5.e4 is the third most common move, played by Akiba Rubinstein, and 5.a4 was seen in Rubinstein–Spielmann, 1922.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- Black gets a strong pawn centre and open lines for the pieces in return for a pawn.
- The open f-file and the bishops on b7 and d6 point at White's kingside.
- After 5.dxe6 fxe6 6.cxb5 d5 White stays a pawn up and can strike at Black's centre with e4.

### Who should play it

It suits Black players of the Benoni and the Benko who meet 1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 e6 3.Nf3 and want an active answer. White can choose between taking the pawn with 5.dxe6 and the more popular 5.Bg5.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 48.7% of the 118,420 games and Black 46.3%, with 5.0% drawn.
