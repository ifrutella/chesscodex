The Benko Gambit (1.d4 Nf6 2.c4 c5 3.d5 b5), also known as the Volga Gambit, is a positional pawn sacrifice. Black gives up a queenside pawn for long-term pressure on the half-open a- and b-files and the long diagonal, and the compensation often lasts deep into the endgame.

### Origins

The idea of ...b5 and ...a6 dates back to the 1930s: Karel Opočenský played it against several opponents from 1936. The name "Volga Gambit" comes from the river Volga: an article about 3...b5 by B. Argunow, written in Kuibyshev, was published in 1946 in the magazine *Shakhmaty v SSSR*. The Hungarian-American grandmaster Pal Benko popularised it from the late 1960s and published *The Benko Gambit* in 1974. Today both names are used. Players who have used it include world champions Mikhail Tal, Garry Kasparov, Viswanathan Anand, Veselin Topalov and Magnus Carlsen, as well as Vasyl Ivanchuk, Alexei Shirov and Boris Gelfand.

### The idea

After 4.cxb5 a6 5.bxa6 Bxa6, Black fianchettoes the dark-squared bishop. For the pawn Black gets fast development, good control of the a1–h8 diagonal and pressure down the half-open a- and b-files, where White's a- and b-pawns can become vulnerable. Exchanging queens often removes the danger of a kingside attack by White, while Black's queenside pressure remains. That is why Benko players are often happy to reach an endgame a pawn down.

### Main lines

- [Fully accepted](/openings/benko-gambit-accepted-fully-accepted-variation) 4.cxb5 a6 5.bxa6 Bxa6: the main line, with Black fianchettoing on g7.
- [Zaitsev System](/openings/benko-gambit-zaitsev-system): 5.Nc3 instead of 5.bxa6.
- [5.b6](/openings/benko-gambit-accepted-pawn-return-variation): White takes the pawn and returns it at once.
- [5.e3](/openings/benko-gambit-accepted-modern-variation): the older half-accepted line, covered in Benko's 1974 book. The newer one is 5.f3, planning a quick e4.
- Declined: [4.Nf3](/openings/benko-gambit-declined-main-line) and 4.a4 (the Sosonko Variation).

### Strengths and weaknesses

**Strengths**

- Clear, long-term plans for Black. Understanding matters more than memorising.
- Good practical chances to play for a win, especially at amateur level.
- The compensation often lasts into the endgame, which is unusual for a gambit.

**Weaknesses**

- Black is a pawn down for good.
- White can decline (4.Nf3, 4.a4) or return the pawn (5.b6) and reach a different kind of game.
- It is only available after 3.d5, so Black needs other lines against 3.Nf3.

### Who should play it

The Benko suits positional players who like long-term pressure and don't mind being a pawn down. It is effective against White players who grab material and then struggle to untangle the queenside. It is less effective against opponents who decline it or give the pawn back early.

### The numbers

The Benko does well for Black on Lichess: from 1400 to 2200, Black wins 51–53% against White's 43–43.5%. Below 1400 it is level (about 48.5% each), and above 2200 White edges ahead 47% to 45%. In master games over the board, White wins 40%, Black 26% and 35% are drawn.
