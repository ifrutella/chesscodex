The Konstantinopolsky Opening is 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6 3.g3, a rarely played line in which White prepares to fianchetto the king's bishop instead of developing it to c4 or b5. The code is C44.

### Origins

According to *The Oxford Companion to Chess*, the opening entered master play in a game between Alexander Konstantinopolsky and Viacheslav Ragozin in a team championship in Moscow in 1956. A slightly earlier example is McKinnon–Walker, U.S. Open Championship, Long Beach 1955, which *Chess Life* magazine called a "King's Indian Reversed". David Bronstein used the name "Konstantinopolsky's opening" in his book *200 Open Games* (published in Russian in 1970), and he played it himself against Levente Lengyel at the 1964 Interzonal in Amsterdam.

### The idea

White often intends to adopt a [King's Indian Attack](/openings/kings-indian-attack) set-up, with the bishop on g2. The opening has been described as a form of "anti-preparation" that takes the game "out of book".

### Strengths and weaknesses

- Black's usual preparation against 3.Bb5, 3.Bc4 or 3.d4 is of no use, and White gets a familiar set-up without much theory.
- The fianchetto is slow, so Black can develop freely and take a share of the centre.
- White can use much the same set-up against most of Black's replies.

### Who should play it

It suits White players who already know the King's Indian Attack or the reversed King's Indian structures and want to use them after 1.e4 e5 2.Nf3 Nc6. Black can answer with natural development, for example ...Nf6, ...d5 or ...Bc5, without having to know a special line.

### The numbers

On Lichess Black does better: White wins 46.0% of 663,558 games and Black 49.4%, with 4.6% drawn. Under 1400 Black wins 48.8% against 46.9%, between 1400 and 1799 50.0% against 45.7%, and between 1800 and 2199 49.2% against 46.0%. From 2200 up White is ahead, 47.7% against 45.2%. In 332 master games White wins 38.6%, Black 22.9%, and 38.6% are drawn.
