Alapin's Opening is 1.e4 e5 2.Ne2, an unusual but perfectly playable line in which White develops the king's knight to e2 and keeps the f-pawn free to advance. The code is C20.

### Origins

The opening is named after Semyon Alapin (1856–1923), the Russo-Lithuanian player and openings analyst. It is rarely used, but it occurred in Jacobsen–Ljubojević, Groningen 1970, and in Hartoch–Ligterink, Amsterdam 1976.

### The idea

White mainly plays it to avoid heavily theoretical lines such as the [Ruy Lopez](/openings/ruy-lopez), or to surprise the opponent. White intends to play f2–f4 soon. If White sets up with g3, Nbc3, d3 and Bg2, the position resembles the Smyslov Position from Smyslov–Botvinnik, 1958.

The knight move has two drawbacks. It blocks the light-squared bishop and the queen, so White needs another knight move or another pawn move to free them, against the principle of developing the minor pieces quickly. And the knight on e2, although flexible, does not control any squares in Black's half of the centre, so it will have to move again to become more useful.

### Main lines

It is relatively easy for Black to equalise: 2...Nf6 and 2...Nc6 both do so, although Black should not be caught out by White's f2–f4 when it comes.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- It takes the game away from known theory at move two and keeps f2–f4 available.
- The knight on e2 shuts in the bishop on f1 and the queen, and it has to move again later.
- Black has an easy route to equality with ordinary developing moves.

### Who should play it

It suits White players who want an offbeat way to reach f2–f4 positions and are content with equality out of the opening. With Black, plain development with ...Nf6 or ...Nc6 is enough, as long as you keep an eye on f2–f4.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 49.1% of 653,980 games and Black 46.7%, with 4.2% drawn. Under 1400 Black is ahead, 50.9% against 45.1%; between 1400 and 2199 White wins 49.0% in both bands. From 2200 up Black wins 48.3% and White 45.7%. Only 65 master games are in the database, too few to judge.
