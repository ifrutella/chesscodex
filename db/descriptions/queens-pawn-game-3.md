After 1.d4 d5 Black answers the queen's pawn with the same move, which starts the family of openings called the Closed Games (also the Double Queen's Pawn Opening). The code is D00. The most frequent second move is 2.c4, the [Queen's Gambit](/openings/queens-gambit); White can also play a set-up such as the London or the Colle and leave the c-pawn at home.

### Origins

1...d5 is Black's traditional reply to 1.d4. The pair of moves became widely popular by the early 20th century, when players with White began to try first moves other than 1.e4 more often. It is still the second most popular answer to 1.d4. The [Indian Defense](/openings/indian-defense), 1...Nf6, is more common now, but it often transposes to the Queen's Gambit or another Closed Game.

### The idea

1.d4 takes a share of the centre and frees the queen's bishop, and 1...d5 does the same for Black. After 1.d4 the pawn on d4 is protected by White's queen, while after 1.e4 the e-pawn stands undefended. Because of this small difference, transpositions between variations are more common than in the king's pawn openings, and the play tends to be more positional, although there are sharp tactical lines too. Playing e2–e4 in one move is harder to support after 1.d4 than d2–d4 is after 1.e4, so White often settles for e3 at some point.

### Main lines

- 2.c4, the Queen's Gambit, is the main move and creates tension at once. Black can take with 2...dxc4, the [Queen's Gambit Accepted](/openings/queens-gambit-accepted), or decline the pawn with 2...e6, the [Queen's Gambit Declined](/openings/queens-gambit-declined), or 2...c6, the [Slav Defense](/openings/slav-defense). Rarer replies are 2...Nc6 ([Chigorin Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-chigorin-defense)), 2...c5 ([Austrian Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-austrian-defense)), 2...Bf5 ([Baltic Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-baltic-defense)), 2...e5 ([Albin Countergambit](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-albin-countergambit)) and 2...Nf6 ([Marshall Defense](/openings/queens-gambit-declined-marshall-defense)).
- 2.Nf3 is the second most common move; the same position comes from 1.Nf3 d5 2.d4. Black usually plays 2...Nf6, and White chooses between 3.c4 (the Queen's Gambit), 3.Bf4 ([London System](/openings/queens-pawn-game-london-system)), 3.Bg5 ([Torre Attack](/openings/queens-pawn-game-torre-attack)), 3.e3 ([Colle System](/openings/queens-pawn-game-colle-system)) and 3.g3, which may turn into the [Catalan](/openings/catalan-opening).
- 2.Nc3 is usually met by 2...Nf6. Then 3.Bg5 leads to the [Richter-Veresov Attack](/openings/richter-veresov-attack) and 3.Bf4 is the [Jobava London](/openings/rapport-jobava-system).
- 2.Bf4 is the [London System](/openings/queens-pawn-game-accelerated-london-system) at once, and 2.Bg5 is the [Hodgson Attack](/openings/queens-pawn-game-levitsky-attack).
- 2.e3 can become the Colle System (Nf3, Bd3, c3, Nbd2, O-O and later e3–e4) or the [Stonewall Attack](/openings/queens-pawn-game-stonewall-attack), with an early f2–f4 and a knight aimed at e5.
- 2.e4 dxe4 3.Nc3 Nf6 4.f3 is the [Blackmar-Diemer Gambit](/openings/blackmar-diemer-gambit), a speculative attacking line.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- White decides how sharp the game will be: 2.c4 starts a fight for the centre at once, while the London, Colle and Stonewall set-ups can be played almost the same way against any defence.
- Those set-ups need little memorising and are common in casual play. Professionals use them less, because Black is thought to equalise against them fairly easily. Ding Liren still beat Ian Nepomniachtchi with the London in the sixth game of the 2023 World Championship.
- The Queen's Gambit Declined and the Slav lead to a huge number of variations, and playing them well can take a lot of opening study.

### Who should play it

1.d4 d5 suits players on either side who like a solid centre and steady positional play. With White you can pick your level of theory, from a fixed system to the main lines of the Queen's Gambit. With Black, 1...d5 is the classical answer; the price is that you have to be ready for 2.c4 as well as the quieter systems.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 51.3% of 275 million games and Black 43.5%, with 5.2% drawn. The split changes little with rating: under 1400 White wins 51.0% and Black 44.8%, and between 1800 and 2199 the figures are 51.2% and 43.3%. From 2200 up the draw rate rises to 8.1%, with White on 49.2% and Black on 42.7%. In 265,860 master games almost half end in a draw (48.6%); White wins 31.9% and Black 19.4%.
