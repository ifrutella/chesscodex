The McDonnell Gambit is 1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.Bc4 g4 5.Nc3, a line of the [King's Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit) in which White leaves the knight on f3 to be taken and brings out another one instead. It can also arise from the [Quaade Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-quaade-gambit), 4.Nc3 g4 5.Bc4. The code is C37.

### Origins

The gambit is named after Alexander McDonnell, the 19th-century Irish master, who introduced it with success in his third match with Louis-Charles Mahé de La Bourdonnais. Their game in London in 1834 went 1.e4 e5 2.f4 exf4 3.Nf3 g5 4.Bc4 g4 5.Nc3 gxf3 6.Qxf3 Bh6 7.d4 Nc6 8.O-O Nxd4 9.Bxf7+ Kxf7 10.Qh5+ Kg7 11.Bxf4 Bxf4 12.Rxf4 Nf6 13.Qg5+ Kf7 14.Raf1 Ke8 15.Rxf6 Qe7 16.Nd5 Qc5 17.Kh1 Ne6 18.Rxe6+ dxe6 19.Nf6+, winning the queen, and La Bourdonnais resigned. 9.Bxf7+ is a frequent idea in the King's Gambit: it removes the pawn that shields Black's king.

### The idea

For the knight White aims at a big lead in development, with the knight, bishop and queen out, and then a kingside attack. The McDonnell Gambit has always been much less popular than the similar [Muzio Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-muzio-gambit-wild-muzio-gambit), 5.O-O. Modern Chess Openings considers it obsolete, but it has not been refuted. It should not be confused with the McDonnell Attack in the [Bishop's Gambit](/openings/kings-gambit-accepted-bishops-gambit).

### Main lines

- 5...gxf3 6.Qxf3 is usual; the wild 6.O-O is occasionally seen. 6...Bh6, as in the McDonnell game, is regarded as inferior.
- 6...d5 7.Nxd5 Nc6 led to approximate equality in Charousek and Fahndrich against Halprin and Marco, Vienna 1897.
- 6...d6 is the other good reply. 7.O-O Be6 8.Nd5 c6 9.Qc3 cxd5 10.Qxh8 dxc4 11.Qxg8 Qb6+ 12.Kh1 Nc6 13.b3 Qd4 is better for Black, according to J. Malkin's analysis in the *Wiener Schachzeitung* of 1911. In Maróczy–Chigorin, Vienna 1903, 7.d4 Be6 8.Nd5 c6 9.O-O cxd5 10.exd5 Bf5 11.Bxf4 Bg6 12.Bb5+ Nd7 13.Rae1+ Be7 14.Bxd6 Kf8? 15.Rxe7 was played, and White won; in Barth–Lenz, correspondence 1913, 14...Qb6! gave Black a clear advantage.
- 5...Nc6 transposes to the [Hamppe-Muzio Gambit](/openings/vienna-game-hamppe-muzio-gambit) after 6.O-O, or to the Pierce Gambit after 6.d4. 5...d5 is also likely to transpose after 6.Bxd5 gxf3 7.Qxf3 or 6.Nxd5 gxf3 7.Qxf3; Marshall–Leonhardt, Hamburg 1911, went 6.Bxd5 gxf3 7.Qxf3 Nf6 8.Qxf4 and White won on move 18.

### Strengths and weaknesses

- White gets fast development and open lines against Black's king for the knight.
- Black has good defences in 6...d5 and 6...d6, and with them Black is at least equal.
- The line is rare, so an opponent is less likely to know it than the Muzio.

### Who should play it

It suits King's Gambit players who like the Muzio's piece sacrifice but want a less analysed version. With Black, take the knight and answer 6.Qxf3 with ...d5 or ...d6 rather than 6...Bh6.

### The numbers

On Lichess White wins 59.4% of the 5,851 games and Black 38.2%, with 2.3% drawn.
